# Angiogram with or without intervention

Source: Hong Kong Children's Hospital, Department of Radiology. Official IR Clinic nursing education leaflet.

Angiogram with or without Intervention

1. What is an angiogram?

An angiogram is an X-ray test used to look at the blood vessels and check for narrowing, blockage, bleeding, or abnormal blood vessel shape. \
A small catheter is usually inserted into an artery, usually through the groin (femoral artery) or wrist (radial artery), and contrast dye is injected so the blood vessels can be seen on X-ray.

If an abnormality is found, the doctor may also treat it during the same session. This is called an intervention, for example ballooning, placing a stent, or other blood vessel treatment depending on the problem.

2. Before the procedure

- Fasting: Follow the fasting instructions given by the anaesthetist if sedation or general anaesthesia is planned. If only local anaesthesia is being planned, your child should have no food, milk, or formula for 4 hours before the procedure.
- Anaesthetic assessment: Attend the pre anaesthesia clinic if sedation or general anaesthesia is planned, as instructed by your healthcare team.
- Admission slip: Please bring the admission slip with you on the procedure day for admission.
- Blood tests: Perform blood tests as instructed by your referring unit, usually within 2 weeks before the procedure.
- Fever or upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) symptoms: Inform the staff if your child develops a fever or any URTI symptoms (such as cough or runny nose) within one week before the procedure; the procedure may need to be delayed.
- Medications: Inform the doctor if your child takes blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines (for example, aspirin, clopidogrel)
- Previous contrast reaction: Inform the doctor if your child has had any previous reaction to contrast media, such as rash, swelling, or breathing difficulty. Steroid premedication may be needed.
- Accompany your child: Please accompany your child on the procedure day and stay with them until they arrive at the examination room.

3. During the procedure

- Position: Your child will lie on the examination table, usually on their back. The area where the catheter will be inserted, usually the groin or wrist, is cleaned and covered in a sterile blanket.
- Anaesthesia/sedation: Most children receive sedation or general anaesthesia so they are asleep and comfortable during the procedure.
- Angiogram: The doctor inserts a thin catheter into an artery and advances it to the area being studied. Contrast dye is then injected through the catheter while X-ray images are taken.
- Intervention, if needed: If a narrowing, blockage, bleeding site, or other problem is found, the doctor may treat it during the same procedure.
- Duration: The procedure may take one to three hours, depending on the area being examined and whether treatment is needed.

4. After the procedure and home care

- Observation: Your child will be monitored in hospital for several hours or overnight after the angiogram or intervention to watch for bleeding and check the puncture site.

- Puncture site care: A dressing will be placed over the puncture site. Keep it clean and dry for the time advised by your team, usually 24 to 48 hours.

- Position after the procedure:

  - If the groin was used, your child may need to lie flat and keep the leg straight for a period of time to reduce bleeding risk.
  - If the wrist was used, your child may need to keep the arm and wrist still for a period of time, and a wrist compression band or device may be used to help stop bleeding.

- Pain: Mild pain or a small bruise at the puncture site is common.

- Activity: Your child should rest for the rest of the day and play quietly the next day. Avoid bike riding, rough play, heavy lifting, and contact sports for about 1 to 2 weeks, or as instructed by your doctor.

- Bathing: Keep the puncture site dry for the first 24 to 48 hours, then follow the instructions given by your team about bathing or showering.

- If an intervention was done, your doctor may give extra instructions about bed rest, limb position, or longer activity restriction.

5. When to seek urgent help

Seek urgent medical care if your child develops:

- If there is heavy bleeding, apply firm pressure to the site and seek emergency help immediately.
- Increasing pain, swelling, redness, or warmth at the puncture site.
- Bruising that is getting larger or feels very firm.
- Numbness, coldness, or colour change in the arm or leg on the same side as the puncture site.
- Fever or a general feeling of being unwell after the procedure.

6. Follow-up

Your child’s doctor or interventional radiology team will explain the results and arrange follow-up if needed.

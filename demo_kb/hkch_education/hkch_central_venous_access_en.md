# Central venous access (PICC, Hickman, port, apheresis line)

Source: Hong Kong Children's Hospital, Department of Radiology. Official IR Clinic nursing education leaflet.

Central Venous Access (PICC, Hickman, Port, Apheresis Line)

1. What is a central venous access line?

A central venous access line is a soft plastic tube that goes into a large vein in your child’s arm, chest, neck, or groin and stays in place for a period of time.\
It is used to give medicines, fluids, nutrition, to take blood, or to perform apheresis treatments without using a needle each time.

- PICC line (Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter): inserted through a vein in the arm and advanced so the tip sits in a large central vein near the heart. The external part of the line comes out of the skin on the arm.
- Hickman line (tunnelled central line): inserted into a vein in the neck, chest, or groin and tunnelled under the skin. The external part of the line comes out of the skin on the chest.
- Port: a small metal or plastic device placed completely under the skin, usually on the chest, with a catheter going into a large vein. When not in use, no needle is in the port; when needed, a needle is placed through the skin into the port.
- Apheresis line: a central line used mainly for apheresis treatments.

2. Before the procedure

- Fasting: Follow the fasting instructions given by the anaesthetist if sedation or general anaesthesia is planned. If only local anaesthesia is being planned, your child should have no food, milk, or formula for 4 hours before the procedure.

- Anaesthetic assessment: Attend the pre anaesthesia clinic if sedation or general anaesthesia is planned, as instructed by your healthcare team.
- Admission slip: Please bring the admission slip with you on the procedure day for admission.

- Blood tests: Perform blood tests as instructed by your referring unit, usually within 2 weeks before the procedure.
- Fever or upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) symptoms: Inform the staff if your child develops a fever or any URTI symptoms (such as cough or runny nose) within one week before the procedure; the procedure may need to be delayed.
- Medications: Inform the doctor if your child takes blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines (for example, aspirin, clopidogrel)

- Previous contrast reaction: Inform the doctor if your child has had any previous reaction to contrast media, such as rash, swelling, or breathing difficulty.
- Accompany your child: Please accompany your child on the procedure day and stay with them until they arrive at the examination room.

3. During the procedure

- Position: Your child will lie on their back on the examination table.

  - For a PICC line, the arm will be placed on a support so the doctor can see the veins in the upper arm.
  - For a Hickman line or port, the neck and chest will be exposed and cleaned.
  - For an apheresis line, the area (chest, neck, or groin) will be exposed and cleaned.

- Anaesthesia/sedation: Local anaesthetic is injected into the skin and deeper tissues so that the area becomes numb. If sedation or general anaesthesia is planned, the anaesthetist will manage this.

- Insertion:

  - For a PICC line, under ultrasound guidance, the doctor inserts the catheter into the vein in the arm and advances it until the tip lies in the correct central vein. The catheter is then secured and covered with a sterile dressing.
  - For a Hickman line, the doctor inserts the catheter into a vein in the neck, chest, or groin, tunnels it under the skin, and brings the external part out through a small incision on the chest. The catheter is secured and covered with a sterile dressing.
  - For a port, the doctor inserts the catheter into a vein in the neck, chest, or groin, tunnels it under the skin, and attaches it to a small port device placed under the skin on the chest (or arm). The incision is closed with stitches or glue and covered with a dressing.
  - For an apheresis line, the doctor inserts the catheter into the vein and advances it until the tip lies in the correct central vein. The catheter is then secured and covered with a sterile dressing.

- Position check: Xray or fluoroscopy is used to confirm that the catheter tip is in the correct position.

- Duration: The procedure itself usually takes within 30 minutes once everything is prepared, though preparation and recovery may take longer.

4. After the procedure and home care

This section is intentionally general because the exact care will depend on your child’s specific device and your team’s protocol.

4.1 General care for all devices

- Observation: Your child will be observed for a short period to ensure there is no significant bleeding, pain, or other problem.
- Pain: When the local anaesthetic begins to wear off, the area may feel bruised and sore. Light painkillers are usually sufficient; avoid aspirin or ibuprofen if advised, as they can increase bleeding.
- Return to school/normal life: Most children can return to school or normal daily activities within 24 hours, unless they were sedated; if sedated, they may be unsteady for a few hours.
- Physical activity: Light activities can be continued as normal. Avoid vigorous exercise, contact sports, or activities that could hit or pull the line.

- Water and bathing:

  - Your child may usually shower after the procedure, but should avoid soaking the area in a bath, hot tub, or swimming pool until the line is removed. If shower, cover the dressing with plastic wrap or use a waterproof cover to protect it from splashes. If the dressing becomes wet, dry it with a clean towel and follow your nurse’s advice about changing it.
  - Swimming is not allowed while your child has a PICC or Hickman line.
  - Dressing care: The PICC/Hickman line dressing must stay clean and dry at all times to prevent infection.

- Line care and flushing: Flushing and cap changes will usually be done by nurses, or parents will be trained if home care is needed.

5. When to seek urgent help

Seek urgent medical care if:

- There is bleeding from the line or port site that does not stop after firm pressure for 5 minutes.
- The line comes out at home; apply pressure to the site for up to 1 minute to stop any bleeding, then cover with a plaster and contact your hospital team immediately.
- The line breaks, leaks fluid, or the tip is not in the correct position.
- There is redness, swelling, pain, warmth, or pus around the line or port site.
- Your child has fever, chills, or appears unusually tired or unwell.

6. Follow-up

- Your child’s doctor will explain what to expect and arrange follow-up as needed.
